# Hooks

The hook is the first 1–2 seconds. On Shorts it is not the most important element of
the clip — it is very nearly the only one, because everything else is only seen by
people the hook already kept.

## What a hook is

A hook is a **specific claim that opens a gap the viewer needs closed.** Three parts:

- **Specific** — a named person, an exact number, a concrete object. Abstractions do
  not create curiosity because there is nothing to be curious about.
- **Claim** — an assertion, not a topic. "Here's what happened with X" announces; "X
  refused, and they launched anyway" asserts.
- **Gap** — after hearing it, something is unresolved. If the hook is self-contained,
  the viewer has already got what they came for.

## Patterns that work

**Paradox / reversal** — the strongest, because the gap is built in.
> "I asked when he started working at NASA. He said: *NASA* started working with *me*."

**Named authority + conflict**
> "Moscow couldn't dictate to NASA. Here's what Carl Sagan said back."

**Concrete danger**
> "How does Georgian tech stop an EV battery from exploding?"

**Confession / vulnerability**
> "Why are we afraid to say 'I don't know'?"

**Absurd specific detail**
> "A cow was running in the tunnel."

## Patterns that fail

These are the shapes that consistently landed in the bottom bucket on the channel this
was built for — an order of magnitude below the examples above:

| Hook shape | Why it dies |
|---|---|
| "\<Technology\> is developing, and \<place\> is falling behind" | Position statement. No gap, no specifics. |
| "\<Event\> changed the future of \<field\>." | Flat assertion about an abstraction. |
| "Your real competitor is time" | Generic motivational. Could be any channel. |
| "Is \<big company\> lying?" | Vague. Lying about what, to whom, how do you know? |
| "\<Industry\> in \<place\> moves to a new stage" | Press-release voice. Nothing at stake. |

The common failure is **commentary instead of event**. Two people discussing a topic is
not a story even when the discussion is intelligent. Look for the moment where someone
*did* something, *was told* something, or *was wrong* about something.

## Writing process

1. Find the single most surprising sentence in the clip. Usually it is not the first
   one — the speaker warms up.
2. Consider moving the clip's start to that sentence. A clip that opens on its own best
   line beats a clip with a caption pasted over a slow start.
3. Write the on-screen hook as **two short lines**, not one long one. Long single lines
   overflow the frame on mobile.
4. Read it as a stranger who has never heard of the channel. If it needs context, it
   is not a hook.

## On-screen vs spoken

The overlay hook and the speaker's opening words should reinforce, not duplicate at
different speeds. If the speaker says the hook line within the first three seconds, an
overlay repeating it verbatim is fine and even helps comprehension on muted autoplay —
most Shorts start silent.

Keep the overlay to ~2.5 seconds. Longer and it covers faces during the part where the
viewer is deciding whether the speaker is worth listening to.

## Titles

The title is a second hook for a different audience — people browsing rather than
scrolling. It can be slightly longer and slightly more explicit than the overlay, and
it should carry a searchable noun (a name, a company, a place) that the overlay may
have left implicit.

Avoid stacking hashtags into the title. They compete with the words that do the work.
